# Peer Review Guide

Peer review runs in Week 12, Project Day, Wednesday 11/11, 3:30 to 6:00 PM, Ridley Hall 137. It is a
journal-style workshop. With four students in the class, the whole group reads for each project in
turn, so every author receives feedback from all three peers and from the instructor.

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **What you bring** | Your current research project draft, either a printed copy or a digital file. Nothing is circulated in advance. |
| **What you do before class** | Nothing is required in advance. Please arrive with the draft you actually want read and one sentence naming the part you are least sure of. |
| **What happens in class** | Opening 3:30 to 3:35, then four 35-minute blocks, one per project, at 3:35, 4:10, 4:50 and 5:25, with a break 4:45 to 4:50. |
| **What you submit** | Nothing. No Canvas submission is required for this activity. |
| **What you leave with** | The strengths, questions, and revision priorities the group named for your project, in your own notes. |

## How a 35-minute block runs

The shape below is a default. The instructor may adjust it for a project that needs something else.

| Minutes | Segment | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 3:00 | **The author frames it** | One or two minutes on what the project is trying to do, then the sentence naming the part you are least sure of. Please do not summarise the whole draft; the group has it in front of them. |
| 3:00 to 12:00 | **Silent reading** | Everyone reads, with the author's flagged section first. Please write as you read. |
| 12:00 to 16:00 | **Read-back** | One reader states, in their own words, what they understood the study to be. The author listens and writes. This is where authors most often discover that what was heard is not what they meant. |
| 16:00 to 30:00 | **Questions and concerns** | Each reader raises what they found, naming the level of the concern. The author listens and writes rather than defending. |
| 30:00 to 35:00 | **Revision priorities** | The group names two or three things to change first, in order. The author writes them down and says which they will start with. |

## Norms for feedback among doctoral peers

Peer review in a doctoral seminar is neither proofreading nor a compliment exchange. You are being asked to do what reviewers, committee members, and collaborators will do to your work for the rest of your career, and to do it well enough to be useful to the author.

**Please read the draft the author wrote, not the one you would have written.** The most common failure in peer review is redesigning someone else's project. If you find yourself proposing a different study, stop and ask instead what would make this study stronger on its own terms. Then, separately and briefly, you may say "have you considered."

**Locate your feedback.** "The framework is weak" is not usable. "The framework section names self-regulated learning on page 3, but the analysis on page 9 only measures activity volume, and I cannot see which construct that volume is standing in for" is usable, because the author knows where to go and what to fix.

**Say what worked, and say why it worked.** This is information for the author rather than politeness. An author who does not know which parts are working will revise the wrong things. Be as specific about strengths as about problems.

**Name the level of your concern.** There is a large difference between "this citation is formatted wrong," "this paragraph is unclear," and "I do not think this design can answer this question." Say which one you are raising. Reviewers who put a structural concern inside a list of comma corrections leave the author unable to tell which comments matter most.

**Ask before you assert.** Much of what looks like an error is a decision the author made for a reason you have not been told. A question prompts the author to state the reason in the draft, where the next reader will need it too.

**Authors: listen first.** In each round, the author's job at the start is to take notes and not defend. You will get time to respond. If you argue in the first minute, the reviewer will hold back for the rest of the round.

**Authors: the revision is yours.** You are not required to take any piece of advice. You are required to be able to say why you did not.
