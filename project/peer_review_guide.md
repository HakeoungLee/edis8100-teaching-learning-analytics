# Peer Review Guide

Peer review runs in Week 12, Project Day, Wednesday 11/11, 3:30 to 6:00 PM, Ridley Hall 137. It is a
journal-style workshop. With three students in the class, the whole group reads for each project in
turn, so each of you reads both of your colleagues' drafts and every author receives feedback from
both peers and from the instructor. The written reviews are drafted and posted during the session,
so nothing is due afterwards.

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **What you bring** | Your current research project draft, either a printed copy or a digital file, and a laptop or tablet, since you post your reviews to Canvas during the session. Nothing is circulated in advance. |
| **What you do before class** | Nothing is required in advance. Please arrive with the draft you actually want read, one sentence naming the part you are least sure of, and a device you can post from. |
| **What happens in class** | An opening on how a referee report is written, on the block clock, and on the norms, 3:30 to 3:40, then three 40-minute blocks, one per project, at 3:40, 4:20 and 5:10, with a break 5:00 to 5:10 and a close on revision commitments 5:50 to 6:00. |
| **What you submit** | A written peer review of each of your two colleagues' projects, two reviews in all, written and posted during class in the last six minutes of that project's own block. Post each one to the Week 12 Peer Review discussion board on Canvas, in the thread for that project. There is one thread per project, three in all, and you post in the two that are not yours; authors may reply in their own thread. The form is a referee report: a short summary paragraph showing what you understood the project to be, then major comments, then minor comments. Nothing is due after the session. The board is visible only to this class, so posting a review there is consistent with keeping the drafts inside the seminar. |
| **What you leave with** | The strengths, questions, and revision priorities the group named for your project, in your own notes. |

## How a 40-minute block runs

The shape below is a default. The instructor may adjust it for a project that needs something else.

| Minutes | Segment | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 3:00 | **Author's framing** | The argument in three sentences, the two places you are stuck, the pages you most want read, and your one question. Please do not summarize the whole draft; the group has it in front of them. |
| 3:00 to 15:00 | **Silent reading and notes** | Nobody speaks. Everyone, the instructor included, reads the flagged pages plus the problem statement, the research questions, and the methodological plan, writing notes against the dimension prompts. These notes become the written review at minute 34, so they are worth writing in sentences rather than fragments. |
| 15:00 to 20:00 | **Read-back** | The lead reader states, in their own words, the argument as received, and names any place where it diverges from what the author said. The author writes. This is where authors most often discover that what was heard is not what they meant. |
| 20:00 to 32:00 | **Dimension feedback** | Around the group: strengths, framework coherence, methods feasibility, ethics and equity, actionability. The author asks clarifying questions only, and writes rather than defending. |
| 32:00 to 34:00 | **The author's one question** | Open conversation on the question the author raised at the start. Everyone takes part. |
| 34:00 to 40:00 | **Write and post your review** | The two reviewers write their review of this project and post it to the discussion board before the block closes. The author writes their own revision priorities in the same six minutes and says which they will start with. Reviewers who took notes on paper hand them over as well. Six minutes is short on purpose: the notes from the silent reading and the dimension round are the draft, and these minutes are for shaping and posting them rather than composing from nothing. |

## Norms for feedback among doctoral peers

Peer review in a doctoral seminar is neither proofreading nor a compliment exchange. You are being asked to do what reviewers, committee members, and collaborators will do to your work for the rest of your career, and to do it well enough to be useful to the author.

**Please read the draft the author wrote, not the one you would have written.** The most common failure in peer review is redesigning someone else's project. If you find yourself proposing a different study, stop and ask instead what would make this study stronger on its own terms. Then, separately and briefly, you may say "have you considered."

**Locate your feedback.** "The framework is weak" is not usable. "The framework section names self-regulated learning on page 3, but the analysis on page 9 only measures activity volume, and I cannot see which construct that volume is standing in for" is usable, because the author knows where to go and what to fix.

**Say what worked, and say why it worked.** This is information for the author rather than politeness. An author who does not know which parts are working will revise the wrong things. Be as specific about strengths as about problems.

**Name the level of your concern.** There is a large difference between "this citation is formatted wrong," "this paragraph is unclear," and "I do not think this design can answer this question." Say which one you are raising. Reviewers who put a structural concern inside a list of comma corrections leave the author unable to tell which comments matter most.

**Ask before you assert.** Much of what looks like an error is a decision the author made for a reason you have not been told. A question prompts the author to state the reason in the draft, where the next reader will need it too.

**Authors: listen first.** In each round, the author's job at the start is to take notes and not defend. You will get time to respond. If you argue in the first minute, the reviewer will hold back for the rest of the round.

**Authors: the revision is yours.** You are not required to take any piece of advice. You are required to be able to say why you did not.
