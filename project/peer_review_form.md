## Norms for feedback among doctoral peers

Peer review in a doctoral seminar is not a proofreading service and it is not a compliment exchange. You are being asked to do the thing that reviewers, committee members, and collaborators will do to your work for the rest of your career, and to do it well enough that the author is glad you did.

**Read the draft the author wrote, not the one you would have written.** The most common failure in peer review is redesigning someone else's project. If you find yourself proposing a different study, stop and ask instead what would make this study stronger on its own terms. Then, separately and briefly, you may say "have you considered."

**Locate your feedback.** "The framework is weak" is not usable. "The framework section names self-regulated learning on page 3, but the analysis on page 9 only measures activity volume, and I cannot see which construct that volume is standing in for" is usable, because the author knows where to go and what to fix.

**Say what worked, and say why it worked.** Not politeness: information. An author who does not know which parts are landing will revise the wrong things. Be as specific about strengths as about problems.

**Name the level of your concern.** There is a large difference between "this citation is formatted wrong," "this paragraph is unclear," and "I do not think this design can answer this question." Say which one you are raising. Reviewers who bury a structural concern inside a list of comma corrections leave the author unable to triage.

**Ask before you assert.** Much of what looks like an error is a decision the author made for a reason you have not been told. A question invites the reason into the draft, where the next reader will need it too.

**Authors: listen first.** In each round, the author's job in the first stretch is to take notes and not defend. You will get time to respond. Defending in the first minute is how a reviewer learns to stop telling you things.

**Authors: you own the revision.** You are not required to take any piece of advice. You are required to be able to say why you did not.

## ⏱️ Timeboxes for the two-round workshop

Both drafts are read in advance and both written forms are complete before you sit down. In-class time is for the conversation, which is the part that cannot be done alone.

### Opening (3:30 to 3:40, 10 minutes)

Protocol overview, pair assignments, and a reminder of the norms above.

### Round 1 (3:40 to 4:40, 60 minutes, in pairs)

| Minutes | What happens |
|---|---|
| 5 | Silent settling. Re-skim your written form, sharpen your one big question. |
| 3 | **Author A frames.** Author A names what they most want feedback on, in under three minutes. Reviewer takes note of it. |
| 15 | **Reviewer speaks, author A listens and writes.** Work through the form in order: strengths first, then framework, methods, ethics and equity, actionability, big question. |
| 7 | **Dialogue.** Author A responds, asks for clarification, tests a possible revision out loud. |
| 3 | Author B frames. |
| 15 | Reviewer speaks, author B listens and writes. |
| 7 | Dialogue. |
| 5 | **Silent revision commitments.** Each author writes down three specific changes they will make. Not "strengthen the framework." Three changes with locations. |

### Break (4:40 to 4:50, 10 minutes)

### Round 2 (4:50 to 5:30, 40 minutes, new partner)

| Minutes | What happens |
|---|---|
| 2 | Settle, re-skim the form. |
| 2 | Author A frames, informed by what round 1 surfaced. |
| 12 | Reviewer speaks, author A listens and writes. |
| 4 | Dialogue. |
| 2 | Author B frames. |
| 12 | Reviewer speaks, author B listens and writes. |
| 4 | Dialogue. |
| 2 | Revision commitments, added to the round 1 list. |

Round 2 is deliberately tighter. Your second reviewer has read the draft cold and did not hear round 1, which makes them the better test of whether the draft communicates without you in the room.

### Instructor mini-conferences (5:30 to 6:00, 30 minutes)

The instructor meets individually while everyone else works through their revision commitments. Bring the two forms and the question you still have.

## 1. Strengths

*What is working, and why? Name at least two things specifically. Point to a page or a section. If one idea in this draft is clearly the author's real contribution, say which one it is, because authors frequently cannot see it and cut it in revision.*

## 2. Framework coherence

*Does the conceptual framework do work in this draft, or does it sit at the front and stop? Trace one construct from the framework section through the research questions to the analysis plan, and report what happens to it. If it disappears somewhere, say where. If the analysis measures something the framework never introduced, say what.*

## 3. Methods feasibility

*Could a person actually do this? Check the chain: data source, access, unit of analysis, cleaning, analysis, output. Name the weakest link. Then say what would have to be added or narrowed for the plan to be executable in the horizon the author has in mind. Be concrete about scale: if the sample, the time frame, or the coding load looks like more than one person can carry, say so now rather than politely.*

## 4. Ethics and equity engagement

*Is this a design section or a disclaimer? Ask of the draft: whose data, on what basis, and who bears the cost of being wrong. Note whether the author has identified a group their measures could systematically misrepresent, and whether the response to that risk changes anything about the design. If the ethics discussion could be pasted into a different proposal without editing, that is the finding to report.*

## 5. Actionability

*Who acts, on what, and when? Say whether you can name the decision and the decision maker after reading the draft. If the contribution is scholarly rather than practical, say whether the conversation it contributes to is identified specifically enough that you could name two people in it.*

## 6. One big question

*The single question that would most improve this project if the author sat with it. One question, not a list. Take a minute to choose it. This is the part of the form authors reread in December.*

## 7. Smaller notes (optional)

*Line-level, citation, APA, and clarity items. Keep these separate from the sections above so the author can triage. If you have annotated the draft itself, write "see draft" here.*

## 🤖 A reminder about documenting AI use

If you used an AI assistant to help you read a peer's draft, to organize your feedback, or to work through your own revision plan, that exchange goes in your Canvas "AI Reflection" upload for week 12 like any other. The policy covers reading and thinking, not only code.

One caution specific to this week: your peers' drafts are their unpublished work. Do not paste a classmate's draft into an AI tool. Comment on it yourself.
