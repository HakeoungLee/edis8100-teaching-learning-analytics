## What this project is

The Course Research Project asks you to apply the theories, methods, and practices of learning analytics to a context that is meaningful to you, and to develop a proposal that could become a future study, a dissertation chapter, an implementation plan, or a publication.

It is a proposal, not a completed study. You are not required to collect data, and you are not required to have results. You are required to make a case: that a problem is worth attention, that a body of literature makes your approach reasonable, that a specific analytic plan would produce evidence about that problem, and that somebody could do something with the evidence.

A strong submission demonstrates all of the following, which are the syllabus expectations verbatim in substance:

1. **A clearly articulated research topic or problem**, situated in a real educational, organizational, or research context.
2. **A conceptual framework** drawn from relevant literature and theory, whether introduced in class or found by you.
3. **A critical and coherent integration** of learning theory with learning analytics tools or approaches. The word to notice is integration: a theory section followed by an unrelated methods section is two documents, not one.
4. **A literature review** that justifies the study and positions it in the field.
5. **A detailed methodological plan**, including data sources and collection strategy, plans for cleaning and preparation, and proposed analysis and visualization methods.
6. **Hypotheses or expected findings.**
7. **Sociotechnical and sociocultural affordances and limitations**, including ethical and equity considerations.
8. **A rationale for actionability**: who could act on this, in what setting, and what would change.

## 📐 Formatting requirements

These come from the syllabus and they are checked.

- **Microsoft Word document.** Not a PDF, not a Google Doc link. The instructor comments in Word.
- **Double spaced**, 11 or 12 point font, **1 inch margins** on all sides.
- **Minimum 20 pages**, excluding references and appendices. Twenty pages of substance, not twenty pages of padding: a proposal that reaches length by restating its problem four times reads exactly as it sounds.
- **All tables and figures labeled**, with sources identified.
- **APA 7 throughout**: heading levels, in-text citations, reference list formatting. The Purdue OWL is the fastest reference when you are unsure.
- **Appendices**, which do not count toward the page minimum, should include:
  - **Code**, if you wrote any. Notebooks from this course count. Include what you ran, not a screenshot of it.
  - **AI interactions**, the full exchange, per the course AI policy.
  - Any additional figures or tables that support but do not belong in the main text.
- **Copyedited.** Read it out loud before you submit. See the checklist.

## 🧭 How the project is graded

One hundred points, eight criteria, each scored at 15, 12, 9, or 6.

| Criterion | Compelling and Scholarly (15) | Clear and Developed (12) | Emerging (9) | Limited (6) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Research Problem and Context** | Compelling, well-situated problem with strong relevance | Clear and meaningful problem statement | Problem is broad or underdeveloped | Problem or context unclear |
| **Conceptual Framework and Theory Integration** | Strong integration of learning theory and analytics | Framework present and connected | Framework is thin or weakly connected | Framework missing or unclear |
| **Literature Review and Positioning** | Synthesizes literature to position the contribution clearly | Reviews relevant literature adequately | Primarily descriptive review | Minimal or unfocused review |
| **Methodological Plan** | Detailed, feasible, and well-justified analytic plan | Clear plan with minor gaps | Plan lacks detail or coherence | Plan unclear or missing |
| **Expected Outcomes and Contribution** | Clearly articulated outcomes and contributions | Outcomes stated clearly | Outcomes vague | Outcomes missing |
| **Ethical, Sociotechnical, and Equity Considerations** | Thoughtful and explicit engagement with ethics and equity | Addresses key considerations | Mentions considerations briefly | Does not address considerations |
| **Actionability and Relevance** | Strong rationale for practical or scholarly use | Actionability described | Actionability vague | Actionability not addressed |
| **Writing Quality and APA Formatting** | Polished, well-organized, APA-accurate writing | Clear writing with minor APA issues | Writing or APA issues affect clarity | Major writing or formatting issues |

Two of these eight criteria, ethics and equity, and actionability, are the ones most often treated as a paragraph near the end. In this course they are load bearing. The seminar spends fourteen weeks arguing that a learning analytics claim is a sociotechnical claim, and a proposal that treats ethics as a compliance appendix has not made that argument.

## 💡 Choosing a topic worth living with

You will be inside this topic from week 9 through finals week, and if it goes well you will be inside it much longer than that. Some honest advice.

**Pick the thing you already cannot stop thinking about.** The syllabus says it plainly: choose a project that genuinely resonates with your interests and goals, one you might want to expand after the course ends, instead of settling for what feels easiest to complete. Easiest to complete is a real feeling in week 9 and a regret in week 14. The projects that go badly are almost never too ambitious. They are the ones the author did not care about.

**A good topic has a real setting attached to it.** "Learning analytics for engagement" is a subject area, not a project. "What would a middle school math department need to see before it would change how it groups students, and could trace data provide it?" is a project, because it names people, a decision, and a place where evidence would land. If you cannot say who would act on your findings, the actionability criterion will be hard to satisfy later, and that is a signal rather than a scoring problem.

**Name a real data source, and say how you would get it.** You are proposing, not collecting, but the plan must be one a person could execute, and that means every milestone from the week 9 outline onward names an actual source: a dataset in a repository, a system held by someone you could ask, or a collection you would run yourself. "LMS data" is a category, not a source. Say which route gets the data to you, whether that is a public download, a free account, a data use agreement, an IRB determination, or an email to an author, and say how long that route takes, because a plan whose data cannot be obtained is not a plan. Say too which columns would stand in for your construct, how large your smallest analytic subgroup is, what is likely to be missing and for whom, and who the data does and does not cover. The course handout *Finding and Evaluating Learning Analytics Data* lists where to look, including the PEERS Data Hub and ICPSR, and ends with a checklist to work through before you commit to a dataset. Naming a source you have some access to, or a system you have worked with, also keeps you honest about what the data would really contain. The notebooks in this repository are a useful sanity check: after week 3 you know what a clickstream actually looks like, and that knowledge makes it much harder to write a methods section that assumes clean, complete, well-labeled traces.

**Your prior work is allowed to count.** A dissertation idea in progress, a problem from your job, a study you keep meaning to run: any of these can become this proposal, as long as the learning analytics contribution is genuinely new work rather than a relabeling of something already written.

**Bring it to the instructor early.** Office hours by request, and the mid-semester check-in in week 6 asks for a project idea seed precisely so that week 9 is not the first time anyone hears your topic. A five-minute conversation in October can save a rewrite in November.

**Signs your topic is the right size**: you can state the problem in two sentences without hedging, you can name at least three studies that would have to be discussed, you can name the dataset or system you would use and the route by which you would get hold of it, you can imagine the figure that would answer your main research question, and you have a specific worry about who could be harmed if the analysis went into production. If you have all five, start writing.

## 🤖 A reminder about documenting AI use

Every milestone in this project requires an AI interaction log, uploaded to the Canvas "AI Reflection" submission for that week. The requirement covers all AI use connected to the work, not only code: understanding a reading, brainstorming a research question, drafting a paragraph, tightening prose. If you used ChatGPT to make sense of a reading and a different tool to draft a summary, both conversations go in.

The instructor is explicit that this is not a judgment of how much you used AI. Evaluation is based on the rubric and on the quality of the final product. Extensive use will not lower your score. Undisclosed use is an Honor Code violation, which is a different matter entirely.

The final draft carries the whole record in an appendix. Saving exchanges as you go is much easier than reconstructing four months of them in finals week.
