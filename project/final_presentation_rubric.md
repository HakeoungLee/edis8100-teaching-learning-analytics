## What the presentation has to cover

From the syllabus, six things:

1. A clear statement of your research problem or implementation focus.
2. The conceptual framework guiding your work.
3. A brief overview of the relevant literature.
4. A description of your proposed data collection and analysis plan.
5. Expected outcomes, hypotheses, or intended impact.
6. Affordances and limitations.

That is a lot for twelve minutes, which is the point. The presentation is not a compressed reading of your paper. It is the argument your paper makes, delivered by someone who has decided what matters most.

## The rubric

One hundred points, five criteria, each scored at 20, 16, 12, or 8.

| Criterion | Engaging and Scholarly (20) | Clear and Organized (16) | Developing (12) | Emerging (8) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem and Purpose Communication** | Clear, compelling articulation of focus and motivation | Problem and purpose are clear | Purpose somewhat unclear | Purpose unclear |
| **Conceptual and Literature Framing** | Concise, coherent framing of theory and literature | Framework and literature covered adequately | Coverage uneven or dense | Framing missing or confusing |
| **Methods and Design Explanation** | Clear, feasible explanation of data and analysis | Methods understandable | Methods under-specified | Methods unclear or missing |
| **Expected Outcomes and Reflection** | Thoughtful discussion of outcomes, affordances, and limits | Outcomes and limits addressed | Brief mention only | Not addressed |
| **Delivery, Timing, and Visual Design** | Confident delivery, excellent pacing, strong visuals | Clear delivery and effective visuals | Uneven delivery or visuals | Disorganized delivery or visuals |

Note what the top level of the first two criteria rewards: **concise** and **coherent**, not comprehensive. A presentation that covers everything at a sprint scores lower than one that covers the argument at a pace people can follow.

## ⏱️ Structuring the twelve minutes

A workable shape, with times. Adjust the proportions to your project, but keep the shape: the audience needs the problem before the framework, and the framework before the methods, or none of it lands.

| Minutes | Segment | What you are doing |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 1:30 | **The problem, in a place** | Name the setting, the people, and what currently happens. Concrete beats general. Do not open with a definition of learning analytics: this room has one. |
| 1:30 to 3:00 | **The framework** | The theory and the two or three constructs you are borrowing, and the sentence that connects them to what the data could show. |
| 3:00 to 4:30 | **Where this sits in the literature** | Two or three strands, what each has established, and the gap or tension you are entering. Three or four citations on the slide, not thirty. |
| 4:30 to 7:30 | **Methods and design** | Your longest segment. Data sources, unit of analysis, cleaning, and the analysis mapped to the research questions. Show the plan as a diagram if you can. |
| 7:30 to 9:30 | **Expected findings** | What you expect, and what would surprise you. If you can sketch the figure that would answer your main question, put it on a slide with labeled axes, even as a hand drawing. |
| 9:30 to 11:00 | **Affordances, limits, ethics, and equity** | What the approach makes visible, what it hides, who it could misrepresent, and what you would do about that. This is a full segment, not a closing caveat. |
| 11:00 to 12:00 | **Contribution and close** | Who could act on this, and what changes if it works. End on a sentence you have written down and rehearsed, not on "so, yeah, that's it." |
| 12:00 to 15:00 | **Questions** | See below. |

**On the three minutes of questions.** Prepare for two or three you expect, and prepare one you are afraid of. When someone asks a question you cannot answer, say so and say what would let you answer it. That answer scores better with a doctoral audience than an improvised defense, and it is also true.

**On timing.** Twelve minutes is short and it is enforced. Rehearse out loud with a timer at least twice, all the way through, because reading slides silently underestimates your real pace by about a third. If you run long in rehearsal, cut a segment rather than speaking faster. The most common cut is the literature strand you are least invested in, and it is usually the right one.

## 🎨 Slide design

**Roughly eight to fourteen slides.** That is about one slide per sixty to ninety seconds. Slides that go by faster than that are not being read, and slides that sit for four minutes have stopped helping.

**Limit the text.** The syllabus asks for slides that are clear, well organized, and limited in text so that key points stand out. A practical version of that: a headline that states the point, and up to three supporting lines. If a slide contains a paragraph, the audience is reading instead of listening, and you are competing with your own slide.

**Make the headline the claim.** "Activity volume predicts achievement weakly" tells the room what to see. "Results" does not.

**Every figure gets labeled axes, a title, and a legend you can read from the back row.** Screenshots of spreadsheets and pasted statistical output are unreadable when projected. Rebuild the one number that matters as a figure, or say it out loud and put it on the slide in large type.

**Design for a projector and a lit room.** Sans serif, at least 24 point body text, strong contrast, and no color distinction that depends on red against green. If your figures come from this semester's notebooks, they already meet that last requirement.

**One slide the room will thank you for**: a single diagram of your design, showing data sources flowing into the unit of analysis and out to the analyses that answer each research question. It does more work than any three bullet slides.

**Bring a backup.** Export a PDF and have it on a flash drive or in your email. Presentation day is the wrong day to discover a font substitution or a dead Wi-Fi connection.

**Number your slides.** It makes question time enormously easier for everyone.
