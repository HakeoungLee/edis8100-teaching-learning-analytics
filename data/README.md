# The EDIS 8100 Data Universe

Everything you analyze this semester comes from two invented places: an undergraduate course at **Blue Ridge University**, and a middle school math game called **FractionQuest**. Neither exists. Every student, every click, every forum post, and every emotion ping in this folder was generated by `generate_all_data.py` with numpy seed 8100.

That is a deliberate choice, and it is worth a minute of your attention before you load a single CSV.

## Why synthetic

Learning analytics runs on data about people who did not usually get to weigh in on being measured. Practicing on real institutional data means practicing on real students, and a course assignment is a poor reason to touch someone's records. So we built a world instead.

The data are synthetic precisely so that we can rehearse the judgment without surveilling anyone. The ask in return is simple: **treat this data as if it were real.** Ask who could be harmed by a claim before you make it. Notice when a metric flattens a person. Notice when your model is confidently wrong about a group. That stance is not a warm-up for the course, it is one of the course's arguments.

You will also find that the data pushes back. Several patterns in here are designed to punish a fast, careless reading. That is on purpose too.

## The setting

### EDUC 1010: Learning How to Learn (weeks 1 to 9 and 11)

A blended undergraduate course at Blue Ridge University, 120 students, running eight instructional weeks from Monday 2026-09-07 through Sunday 2026-11-01.

| Piece of the course | What it produces |
|---|---|
| Canvas-style LMS | `lms_clickstream.csv` |
| Eight weekly quizzes plus a final project | `gradebook.csv` |
| Threaded discussion forum, one topic per week | `forum_posts.csv` |
| 24 studio groups of 5, meeting Thursdays 4:00 to 5:30 PM | `group_chat.csv`, `mmla_studio.csv`, `studio_artifacts.csv` |
| An adaptive practice tutor | `srl_traces.csv` |
| The roster itself | `students.csv` |

Weekly forum and studio topics run in this order: memory, spacing, metacognition, motivation, collaboration, note-taking, sleep, test anxiety.

Quiz *k* covers the material of week *k* and is due at 23:59 on the Tuesday after that week closes, so deadlines land on 9/15, 9/22, 9/29, 10/6, 10/13, 10/20, 10/27, and 11/3. The final project is due 2026-11-10. The clickstream export ends on 11/1, which is why the last quiz deadline falls just outside it. Real LMS exports have boundaries like this, and noticing the boundary is part of the work.

### FractionQuest (week 10)

A fractions game played by 200 middle schoolers in grades 6 and 7 during October 2026. Eight levels, a pre-test and post-test out of 20, attempt-by-attempt telemetry, and in-game emotion self reports.

| Piece of the game | What it produces |
|---|---|
| The roster and the assessments | `game_players.csv` |
| Attempt level play log | `game_telemetry.csv` |
| In-game emotion pings | `game_emotion.csv` |

## Instructor notes: the planted phenomena

The rest of this document is for the instructor. It names what was deliberately built into the data, where it lives, and which week is designed to surface it. Student-facing task text never states these; the whole point is that students find them.

Every effect is noisy on purpose. Standardized differences sit roughly between 0.4 and 0.8, correlations where the design calls for weak or moderate sit roughly between 0.25 and 0.5, and nothing in here is a perfect relationship. `verify_phenomena.py` asserts all of this and fails loudly if a change to the generator flattens or exaggerates an effect.

| ID | Phenomenon | Where it lives | Week that surfaces it |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Activity volume relates only weakly to achievement, and a visible cluster of efficient students posts low click counts with high scores | `lms_clickstream.csv` plus `gradebook.csv` | Week 2, previewed in week 1 |
| P2 | An at-risk model built on activity features over-flags first generation students working 15+ hours; a model built on prior GPA does not | `lms_clickstream.csv`, `students.csv`, `gradebook.csv` | Week 3, revisited in week 4 |
| P3 | Submitting within 6 hours of the deadline goes with lower quiz scores | `gradebook.csv` | Week 8, teased in week 2 |
| P4 | The reply network holds three loose clusters bridged by four connectors with high betweenness and unremarkable degree | `forum_posts.csv` | Week 8 |
| P5 | Multilingual students speak less in studio and contribute as much or more through chat, ideas, and document edits | `mmla_studio.csv`, `group_chat.csv` | Week 6 |
| P6 | Complete SRL loops go with steeper quiz-to-quiz growth; a hint-spam subgroup grows least | `srl_traces.csv` plus `gradebook.csv` | Week 7 |
| P7 | Productive strugglers gain more than error-free fast finishers, and level 4 is a difficulty cliff | `game_telemetry.csv`, `game_players.csv` | Week 10 |
| P8 | Confusion that resolves within two levels predicts gains; unresolved confusion precedes quitting | `game_emotion.csv`, `game_telemetry.csv` | Week 10 |
| P9 | Groups with balanced talk produce higher rated artifacts than dominated groups | `mmla_studio.csv`, `studio_artifacts.csv` | Week 6, revisited with chat in week 9 |

### P1: volume is not learning

Total clickstream events correlate with mean quiz score at about r = 0.34. Real, visible on a scatterplot, and far too weak to carry the story students expect. Eighteen students were built as efficient: high ability, roughly one third the click volume of their peers. Eight of them land in the bottom third of activity and the top quarter of scores at once, which is enough to see as a cluster and not enough to dismiss as noise.

The discussion this is for: what does a scatterplot of activity against achievement actually license you to say?

### P2: the bias is in the feature, not the algorithm

Thirty students are first generation and work 15 or more hours a week. They study in concentrated bursts, so their **regularity** collapses (a median of 7 active days across the term against 30 for everyone else) while their total event count stays ordinary and their grades stay ordinary.

Regularity genuinely predicts achievement for the other 90 students, so a logistic regression on activity features learns to treat few active days as danger. Result: a model with 75 percent accuracy and a false positive rate of about 0.24 inside that group against 0.03 everywhere else. Swap the features for prior GPA and the accuracy goes up while the gap drops to about 0.08.

The point to land: nothing was wrong with the algorithm. The design decision about which features to use is where the harm entered, and design decisions can be revised. This is the notebook that pairs with Tsai and Martinez-Maldonado, Holstein and Doroudi, and Lee and Gargroetzi.

### P3: correlation, and a chance to say so out loud

About 22 percent of quiz submissions arrive in the final 6 hours, and those submissions score lower by roughly d = 0.69. In the generator, procrastination lowers scores and shortens submission lead time separately. Nothing about submitting late causes the lower score. Students who propose a deadline-nudge intervention here have made a reasonable and unwarranted leap, which is a good thing to discover in week 8 rather than in a dissertation.

### P4: the bridge builders

Students belong to one of three conversational clusters, and 95 percent of reply edges stay inside a cluster, so greedy modularity finds the communities easily. Four students were built to answer people in the other two clusters, each keeping up ties with three regular partners elsewhere. All four land in the top five by betweenness while sitting around the 65th percentile of degree.

Ask what would happen to this forum if those four students stopped posting, and then ask whether any dashboard the students have seen would have noticed them.

### P5: participation looks different across modalities

Twenty-four multilingual students hold the floor less in studio (d = -0.59 on speaking time) and contribute more through the backchannel: idea units d = +0.52, document edits d = +0.41, message counts d = +0.24. A single-modality measure of participation gets these students exactly backwards.

This is the dataset that echoes the instructor's own work on multimodal participation. The stretch goal, building a fairer composite index, is where the interesting arguments happen.

### P6: loops beat hint spam

A student's loop rate, the share of tutor sessions containing `set_goal` before `reflect`, correlates with their quiz-to-quiz slope at about r = 0.52. Eighteen students fire `request_hint` three to eight times in a row with gaps of a few seconds; their mean slope is the lowest of any group (d = -0.54 against everyone else), and the ordering runs hint-spammers, then middle, then loopers.

Detecting the hint spam requires looking at time gaps, not action counts, which is the methodological point of the week 7 lab.

### P7: productive struggle, and the cliff at level 4

Level 4 introduces unlike denominators. Mean errors jump to 5.3 against 2.8 at level 3 and 3.3 at level 5, mean time nearly doubles, and the per-attempt completion rate drops from about 0.71 to 0.50. Attrition concentrates there.

Players who make moderate early errors and keep retrying gain about d = 0.74 more from pre to post than the error-free fast finishers, who started higher and had less room to move. The ceiling effect is real in the data, and separating "gained less" from "had less to gain" is the analytic move worth teaching.

### P8: confusion is not the problem, unresolved confusion is

Among players who ever reported confusion, those whose confusion disappears within two levels gain about d = 0.45 more than those whose confusion persists. The persistent group also quits at 76 percent against 40 percent. Reading the emotion stream as a sequence rather than as a frequency table is the whole exercise.

### P9: balanced talk, better products

Per group and session, the Gini coefficient of speaking time correlates with the artifact rubric score at about r = -0.33. The most balanced third of sessions outscores the most dominated third by about d = 0.69. Group ability and the volume of substantive chat also matter, so a student who claims that equalizing talk time would raise scores has overreached, and can be asked to say what else would have to be true.

Week 9 returns to this with chat response latencies and per-group sociograms, and asks the design question directly: what should a collaboration dashboard refuse to show?

## Regenerating the data

```bash
# from this folder
python generate_all_data.py                 # writes all 11 CSVs next to the script
python generate_all_data.py --outdir /tmp/x # or somewhere else
python verify_phenomena.py                  # PASS or FAIL per phenomenon, nonzero exit on failure
```

Requirements are numpy and pandas, nothing else. A full run takes about one second.

Both scripts were tested with `/opt/anaconda3/bin/python3` (Python 3.12, numpy 1.26, pandas 2.2).

### How the generator is put together

One master seed, 8100. Each dataset function seeds its own generator with 8100 plus a fixed offset, so any single file can be rebuilt on its own and comes out byte identical every time.

| Function | Offset | Writes |
|---|---|---|
| `make_students` | +0 | `students.csv` |
| `make_clickstream` | +1 | `lms_clickstream.csv` |
| `make_gradebook` | +2 | `gradebook.csv` |
| `make_forum` | +3 | `forum_posts.csv` |
| `make_group_chat` | +4 | `group_chat.csv` |
| `make_mmla` | +5 | `mmla_studio.csv` |
| `make_studio_artifacts` | +6 | `studio_artifacts.csv` |
| `make_srl` | +7 | `srl_traces.csv` |
| `make_game_players` | +10 | `game_players.csv` |
| `make_game_telemetry` | +11 | `game_telemetry.csv` |
| `make_game_emotion` | +12 | `game_emotion.csv` |

`make_students()` computes every latent trait for the 120 students: ability, engagement, self regulated learning skill, procrastination, regularity, talkativeness, and the group memberships and subgroup flags that carry the planted phenomena. Those latents are returned in the DataFrame and never written to `students.csv`, because students in the course are meant to discover the structure rather than read it off a column. Every other function calls `make_students(write=False)` to get them, which is why the files agree with each other without any of them having to run first.

`make_game_players()` plays the same role for FractionQuest.

Two functions read another dataset rather than a latent: `make_studio_artifacts` calls `make_mmla(write=False)` because artifact quality depends on the speaking time actually generated, and `make_game_emotion` calls `make_game_telemetry(write=False)` because pings have to land inside real play sessions. Neither writes a second file.

### If you change the generator

Run `verify_phenomena.py` before committing. It checks correlation magnitudes, the direction and size of the false positive rate gap, cluster and group mean differences, the Gini to artifact relationship, and the pre to post gain comparisons. It exits nonzero if any single phenomenon has drifted out of the range the notebooks depend on.

The week 1 through 11 notebooks each embed a copy of the functions they need, so a Colab runtime can build its own CSVs with no network access. If you change a generator function here, update the embedded copy in any notebook that uses it, then re-run that notebook end to end.
